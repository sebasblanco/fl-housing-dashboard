The core relationship

Two mechanics explain almost every price cycle, including 2008–09. Everything in the tiles below is tagged by which one it feeds.

↕Inverse to price

When this indicator rises, prices tend to fall — and vice versa. These are the supply-and-cost-side levers.

  • Mortgage rates ↑ → monthly payment ↑ → buyer pool shrinks → prices soften
  • Months of supply ↑ → sellers compete for fewer buyers → prices soften
  • Days on market ↑ → buyer leverage ↑ → prices soften
  • Insurance / HOA cost ↑ → effective price ↑ → demand shrinks at the same list price
⇄Proportional to price

When this indicator rises, prices tend to rise with it — these are demand-side confirmations.

  • Sales volume / pending contracts ↑ → prices firm up or accelerate
  • Net in-migration ↑ → more buyers chasing the same stock → prices firm
  • Wage growth ↑ → affordability improves → market can sustain higher prices
  • New construction permits ↑ → future supply ↑ → cools *future* price growth (lagged inverse)

Reading the whole board

The headline change since August isn't supply or demand — it's rates, and they moved the wrong way for buyers, climbing from 6.66% to a 7.32% peak in late October before easing slightly to 7.24% (Oct 29), still within half a point of the 2023 high. That undercuts the "rates drift back toward 5–5.5%" path this dashboard was tracking, even as every supply-side metric (days on market, price cuts, months of supply) still reads buyer-favorable. The biggest single surprise in the city data: Fort Lauderdale (+14.1% YoY) has pulled ahead of Miami as Florida's most expensive major market, something that wasn't true in August. Florida condos remain the clearest 2008-style distress signal on the board, but that story is actually improving — supply eased from 9.3 to 7.7 months — with one real near-term risk: a wave of forced listings as the SB 4-D special-assessment compliance deadline (Dec 31, 2026) hits under-reserved associations. No subprime/ARM-reset wave, no collapse in sales volume, and current owners are still largely locked into low rates rather than forced sellers — the 2008 comparison remains a condo-insurance story, not a credit one.
